## PART 2: Lecture 6, Utilitarianism (connecting material)

### A. Core concepts
- **Consequentialism:** an act is required *just because* it produces the best overall results (is **"optimific"**). The term was coined by **Anscombe**, "Modern Moral Philosophy" (1958) (L6 s5).
- **Bentham:** quantitative hedonistic act-utilitarianism. Uses the **hedonic calculus** with seven dimensions: intensity, duration, certainty, propinquity, fecundity, purity, extent (L6 s8).
- **Mill:** qualitative utilitarianism, with higher and lower pleasures (L6 s13; L5 s31–36).
- **Act utilitarianism (AU):** intrinsic value is well-being, and the right act is whichever improves overall well-being (L6 s18).
- **Rule utilitarianism (RU):** an act is right *because it is required by an optimific social rule*, i.e., a rule such that *if everyone in a society accepted it, the results would be optimific* (L6 s19; Brandt, Hooker). Pop-quiz Q2 tests the three-step RU test.

### B. Arguments and objections
- **Attractions of AU** (L6 s20): impartiality, justification, advocacy, moral flexibility, moral community.
- **Difficulties** (L6 s12, s21):
  1. **Quantification.** How do you add up pleasures? Marx called utilitarianism a philosophy for "bean counters".
  2. **Foresight / counterfactuals.** You must predict the future (Manhattan Project).
  3. **Over-demanding.** Singer erases the line between **supererogation** and **obligation**.
  4. **Justice, desert, rights.** The **patient's dilemma** and the trolley.
  5. **Decision procedure vs standard of rightness.** A defender can say AU is only a *standard*, not a method of deciding.
  - It also justifies majoritarianism, slavery and torture (L6 s10).
- **Smart's "rule worship" objection to RU** (L6 s22–25): RU either collapses into AU (once it allows exceptions) or becomes deontology in disguise. Smart asks: *"is it not monstrous to suppose that if… we know that in this instance to break R will have better results than to keep it, we should nevertheless obey the rule? … Is not this a form of superstitious rule-worship…?"* (J.J.C. Smart, "Extreme and Restricted Utilitarianism", 1956, quoted L6 s25)
- **Effective altruism and longtermism** (L6 s26–33): MacAskill; the Gebru & Torres "TESCREAL" critique. Counterpoints from Hoole: Ida Scudder and CMC Vellore show value "beyond calculation", and altruism can "distort community".

### C. Quotations
- Bentham: *"Nature has placed mankind under the governance of two sovereign masters, pain and pleasure. It is for them alone to point out what we ought to do, as well as to determine what we shall do."* (*Introduction to the Principles of Morals and Legislation*, 1780, as quoted on L6 s7.) Pop-quiz Q20 tests this.
- Mill: *"It is better to be a human being dissatisfied than a pig satisfied; better to be Socrates dissatisfied than a fool satisfied."* (*Utilitarianism*, 1863, L6 s13 / L5 s34)
- Singer: *"we ought to give the money away, and it is wrong not to do so."* ("Famine, Affluence, and Morality", 1971, L6 s16)

### D. Why you need this for the end-sem
Every Kant and Aristotle answer is stronger with a one-line contrast to utilitarianism:
- **Kant:** consequences don't make an act right. Even the **patient's dilemma** is answered by the humanity formula: the healthy man is being used *merely as a means*.
- **Aristotle:** virtue is not a calculation. *"Matters concerned with conduct… have no fixity"*, so no algorithm like the hedonic calculus can replace phronesis.
- **Engineering (L9):** Bovens's "consideration of the consequences" and the human-welfare ideal are explicitly labelled "Recall consequentialism / utilitarianism" (L9 s21, s25).

## PART 3: Lecture 7, Kant's Deontology (with reading LE pp. 86–93)

### A. Conceptual understanding, step by step

**1. A theory of the right.** Right comes before good. Kant judges an act by its **motive and maxim**, not its results (L7 s5–6). *Deontology* comes from *deon*, duty: "duty-based" (L7 s8).

**2. The good will** is the only thing good *without limitation* (Kant, LE p.86). Talents of mind (understanding, wit, judgment), temperament (courage, resolution) and gifts of fortune (power, riches, honour, health, even *happiness*) can all become *"extremely evil and harmful"* without a good will. Kant's example: *"the coolness of a scoundrel makes him not only far more dangerous but also immediately more abominable"* (LE p.87). This is a direct attack on hedonism: happiness is **not** always good (LE p.93, study Q2).

**3. Results don't add to the good will's worth.** Even if it achieves nothing, *"like a jewel, it would still shine by itself, as something that has its full worth in itself. Usefulness or fruitlessness can neither add anything to this worth nor take anything away from it."* (LE p.87)

**4. Acting *from* duty vs acting *in conformity with* duty.** Only the first has moral worth (LE pp.87–88; L7 s21). Kant's four cases:

| Case (LE pp. 87–88) | Conforms to duty? | Done from duty? | Moral worth? |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Shopkeeper** who doesn't overcharge a child, because *"his advantage required it"* | Yes | No, self-interest | **No** |
| Ordinary person preserving their life out of inclination | Yes | No | **No** (*"no inner worth"*) |
| Despairing man who *"preserves his life without loving it… from duty"* | Yes | Yes | **Yes** |
| Naturally sympathetic **philanthropist** | Yes | No, inclination | **No** ("deserves praise and encouragement but not esteem") |
| The same philanthropist, grief-stricken, who helps *"simply from duty"* | Yes | Yes | **Yes**, *"then the action first has its genuine moral worth"* |

**5. A maxim** is *"the subjective principle of acting"*: the rule I actually act on. It differs from the objective practical *law* that holds for every rational being (Kant's footnote 1, LE p.90). The slides add: *"It states what you are about to do and why you are about to do it"* (L7 s12).

**6. Hypothetical vs categorical imperatives** (LE p.89; L7 s9):
- **Hypothetical:** "If you want X, do Y." It binds only if you want X. In Kant's words it presents an action *"as a means to achieving something else that one wills."*
- **Categorical:** commands *"an action as objectively necessary of itself, without reference to another end."* **Moral duties are categorical** (pop-quiz Q7). *"Cheating is wrong"* is categorical; *"if you don't want to get caught, don't cheat"* is hypothetical (pop-quiz Q16).

**7. Formula of Universal Law (FUL).** *"act only in accordance with that maxim through which you can at the same time will that it become a universal law."* (LE p.90). The law-of-nature version: *"act as if the maxim of your action were to become by your will a universal law of nature."* (LE p.90)

**The three-part test** (L7 s13):
1. Formulate the maxim clearly.
2. Imagine a world where everyone acts on it.
3. Ask whether your goal can still be achieved in that world.

**8. Fairness and consistency** (L7 s8, s13; pop-quiz Q5: *"consistency and fairness"* are the two criteria of universalizability):
- **Fairness:** you make no exception for yourself. Kant: *"we take the liberty of making an exception to it for ourselves (or just for this once)"* (LE p.91). L7 s8 calls it unfair *"that one is getting away with making an exception to the rule while expecting others to adhere to it."*
- **Consistency:** your goal must stay achievable when everyone acts on the same maxim. Universal tax-cheating, plagiarism or infidelity would make welfare, assessment and marriage meaningless (L7 s8).
- **Therefore:** *"immoral conduct is irrational!"* (L7 s13, s17).

**9. Kant's four examples** (LE pp. 90–92). Know these and which test each one fails:

| Example | Type of duty | FUL verdict (LE pp. 90–91) | FH verdict (LE p. 92) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. **Suicide** from self-love | Perfect, to oneself | A nature that destroys life through the very feeling meant to preserve it *"would contradict itself"* | Uses one's own person *"merely as a means to maintain a tolerable condition"* |
| 2. **False promise** to borrow money | Perfect, to others | Universalized, it makes *"the promise… itself impossible, since no one would believe what was promised"*. Contradiction **in conception** | The lender *"cannot possibly agree to my way of behaving toward him"* |
| 3. **Neglecting talents** (the "South Sea Islanders" example) | Imperfect, to oneself | Such a world *could* exist, but a rational being *"cannot possibly will"* it. Contradiction **in the will** | Fails to *further* humanity's predispositions to perfection |
| 4. **Refusing to help** others ("what is it to me?") | Imperfect, to others | The world could exist, but *"a will that decided this would conflict with itself"*, because you'll need help yourself | Only *"negative"* agreement with humanity: you must *"further the ends of others"* |

*Kant's footnote 2 (LE p.90): "a perfect duty [is] one that admits no exception in favor of inclination."* The labels "contradiction in conception" and "contradiction in will" are standard terms for this distinction. The reading itself says "could not even be thought" vs "impossible to will" (*paraphrase*). Use the labels as interpretation, not as Kant's own words.

**10. Formula of Humanity (FH).** *"So act that you use humanity, whether in your own person or in the person of any other, always at the same time as an end, never merely as a means."* (LE p.92)
- **Why it's needed** (L7 s14): the slides note FUL is *"compatible with slavery or tyranny of the majority"*, so the humanity principle is added as *"a further condition"*.
- **Ground:** *"rational beings are called **persons** because their nature already marks them out as an end in itself"*. Non-rational beings *"have only a relative worth, as means, and are therefore called **things**"* (LE p.91).
- **"Merely":** Kant does not forbid all use of others. I use a bus driver as a means to get home, but I don't treat them *merely* as a means, because they consent to the arrangement and could share its end. The test is whether the other person *"could possibly agree"* and *"contain in himself the end"* of my action (LE p.92).

**11. Autonomy** (L7 s15): we are ends because we can, by reason, *create our own ends* instead of just following instinct. *Autos* (self) + *nomos* (law) means **self-legislators**. Pop-quiz Q9: the moral law is given *by one's own will*, not by society, God or nature.

**12. FUL and FH are two expressions of one principle** (L7 s16): following universalizability *implies* treating people as autonomous ends.

### B. Kant and the trolley problem (L7 s19–31)
- **Intended vs merely foreseen effects:** we do *x* while intending *y*. Examples: splashing pedestrians while driving through a puddle; Jevons paradox; facial recognition replicating bias (L7 s19–20).
- **The Knobe effect** (L7 s22–24; pop-quiz Q17): people judge a *bad* side effect to be brought about *intentionally*, and a *good* side effect *not* intentionally, even though the chairman's state of mind is the same in both cases.
- **Doctrine of Double Effect (DDE):** *"it is sometimes permissible to bring about by oblique intention what one may not directly intend"* (Foot, as quoted on L7 s30). It is built into law (murder vs manslaughter), medicine and the rules of war.
- **Trolley vs patient's dilemma** (pop-quiz Q12):
  - AU sees no difference.
  - In the trolley case you **foresee** but don't intend the death of the one. In the hospital case you **intend** the healthy patient's death.
  - On Kantian grounds (the pop-quiz key's answer), both are wrongful.
- **Foot's alternative: negative vs positive duties** (L7 s31):
  - Negative duties: not to harm. Positive duties: to help. **Negative duties outweigh positive ones.**
  - In the trolley case you redirect an *existing* threat. In the hospital case you *introduce* a new one.
- **Pioneers** (L7 s26): **Foot** introduced the trolley in 1967 (pop-quiz Q14). **Anscombe** coined "consequentialism" in 1958. **Judith Jarvis Thomson** named "the trolley problem" and added the fat man.

### C. Objections to Kant (L7 s32–33; LE p.93 study questions)
1. **Are we as free and autonomous as Kant thinks?** Psychological and social constraints (compare vdP's freedom condition).
2. **Animals.** On FH, non-rational beings are "things", so Kant seems unable to explain why cruelty to animals is wrong. Utilitarians (Bentham's "extent", Singer) can.
3. **Severe consequences.** Indifference to results can be monstrous (e.g., refusing to lie to save a life). Kant also justifies capital punishment.
4. **Perfect duties are exceptionless,** so what happens when duties conflict? (W.D. Ross's *prima facie* duties, L9 s12, are the classic fix.)
5. **"The functionalism of duty."** *"Would you rather have someone do something out of excitement and willingness, or just out of duty?"* (L7 s32). Kant seems to rank the cold dutiful man above the warm philanthropist. This is a key **Aristotle vs Kant** contrast: Aristotle says the virtuous person *delights* in acting well (LE p.144–145).
6. **The test lets some cases through wrongly** (LE p.93 Q5): trivial maxims fail ("I'll play tennis Sunday mornings when the courts are empty") and immoral ones pass if worded narrowly enough. *(These examples are standard illustrations of the objection, not from the slides.)*
7. **AI and robots** (L7 s21, s33): machines have *functions* but do they have *intentions*? Can they act "from duty"? If robots became rational and autonomous, could we still treat them as mere means?

### D. Reading guide: Kant, LE pp. 86–93
- **Thesis:** morality rests on **reason, not consequences or inclination.** The only unconditional good is a good will acting from duty. Duty's supreme principle is the categorical imperative, in two formulations (FUL and FH).
- **Its structure:** good will (p.86–87) → duty vs inclination (pp.87–88) → the law that a good will follows: universal conformity (p.88) → false-promise test (pp.88–89) → hypothetical vs categorical (p.89) → FUL and four examples (pp.90–91) → persons vs things (p.91) → FH and the same four examples (p.92).
- **Where to use it:** every Kant answer; any "why not utilitarianism" point; any "is happiness always good" point; moral vs professional conflict (Boisjoly's duty not to deceive).

## PART 4: Lecture 8, Aristotle and Virtue Ethics (with reading LE pp. 143–150)

### A. Conceptual understanding
**1. The question changes.** Virtue ethics shifts from "what is right or wrong?" to **"how ought we to live our lives?"** We don't get right action by calculating or rule-checking. We get it by **becoming the right kind of person**: *"Try to become a virtuous person, and good actions will follow."* (L8 s6). The person is evaluated, not the isolated act (L8 s10).

**2. Three components:** virtue, practical wisdom, eudaimonia (L8 s8).

**3. Eudaimonia and the function argument** (L8 s9):
- *Eudaimonia* is best translated **"flourishing"**: flourishing *in and through life*, not mere pleasure or success in one area.
- A thing's good lies in fulfilling its **function** (ergon): the knife's function is to cut, the carpenter's to make lasting furniture.
- The human function is **rationality**. Humans are *rational animals*.
- The reading makes the same link: *"every virtue or excellence both brings into good condition the thing of which it is the excellence and makes the work of that thing be done well; e.g. the excellence of the eye…"* So human virtue is *"the state of character which makes a man good and which makes him do his own work well."* (LE p.146)

**4. Two kinds of virtue: how each is acquired** (LE p.144; L8 s12). **This was 2024 Q4.**
> *"intellectual virtue in the main owes both its birth and its growth to **teaching** (for which reason it requires experience and time), while moral virtue comes about as a result of **habit**"* (LE p.144). The Greek *ēthikē* (moral) is a slight variation of *ethos* (habit). *(The Greek words are lost in this PDF. The derivation from "habit" is in the text.)*

**5. Virtue is neither natural nor contrary to nature.** A stone thrown up *"ten thousand times"* can't be habituated to rise. *"Neither by nature, then, nor contrary to nature do the virtues arise in us; rather we are adapted by nature to receive them, and are made perfect by habit."* (LE p.144)

**6. Learning by doing (the analogy with the arts).** With natural capacities like sight, we have the capacity first and use it later. Virtues and arts are the reverse: *"we get by first exercising them… men become builders by building and lyre-players by playing the lyre; so too we become just by doing just acts, temperate by doing temperate acts, brave by doing brave acts."* (LE p.144)

**7. Ethics is practical and inexact.** *"we are inquiring not in order to know what virtue is, but in order to become good"* (LE p.144). *"matters concerned with conduct and questions of what is good for us have no fixity, any more than matters of health… the agents themselves must in each case consider what is appropriate to the occasion, as happens also in the art of medicine or of navigation."* (LE p.144). The slides call this **particularist, contextual, situation-relative** ethics (L8 s14). It is the opposite of Kant's universal law and Bentham's calculus.

**8. The health and strength analogy, and the virtuous cycle.** Too much or too little exercise and food destroys strength and health. In the same way, *"the man who flies from and fears everything… becomes a coward, and the man who fears nothing at all… becomes rash"* (LE p.144). Virtues are **preserved by the mean.** And the cycle reinforces itself: *"by abstaining from pleasures we become temperate, and it is when we have become so that we are most able to abstain from them"* (LE p.145; L8 s16).

**9. Pleasure as the sign of character.** *"the man who abstains from bodily pleasures and delights in this very fact is temperate, while the man who is annoyed at it is self-indulgent"* (LE p.144). Hence moral education: *"we ought to have been brought up in a particular way from our very youth, as Plato says, so as both to delight in and to be pained by the things that we ought; this is the right education."* (LE p.145)
- **Contrast with Kant:** for Kant, acting *against* inclination shows moral worth. For Aristotle, acting well *while still pained* shows you aren't yet fully virtuous. Shafer-Landau calls that state *continence* (LE p.142–143, "Essential Concepts").

**10. The circularity puzzle and the three conditions of virtuous action** (LE pp.145–146; L8 s18–19).
- **The puzzle:** if we become just by doing just acts, aren't we already just?
- **The answer:** the arts and the virtues differ. In art, the *product* carries the goodness. With virtue, the *agent* must be in a certain condition:
  1. **Knowledge.** *"he must have knowledge"*
  2. **Choice.** *"he must choose the acts, and choose them for their own sakes"*
  3. **Firm character.** *"his action must proceed from a firm and unchangeable character"* (LE pp.145–146)
- Then: *"it is not the man who does these that is just and temperate, but the man who also does them as just and temperate men do them."* (LE p.146). The slides (L8 s18–19) mark this "Against consequentialism?", because the same outward act can be virtuous or not depending on the agent.

**11. The definition of virtue: genus and differentia** (LE pp.146–147; L8 s20–23):
- **Genus.** The soul contains *passions, faculties, states of character* (LE p.146). Virtue is **not a passion**: we aren't praised or blamed for feeling anger, passions involve no choice, and we are "moved" by passions but "disposed" by virtues. Virtue is **not a faculty**: faculties come by nature. So virtue is a **state of character** (*hexis*).
- **Differentia.** It is a disposition to choose **the mean**.
- **Mean in the object vs relative to us:** the arithmetic mean of 10 and 2 is 6. But a trainer won't prescribe 6 pounds of food to everyone, because that is *"too little for Milo, too much for the beginner"* (LE p.147).
- The mean is to feel and act *"at the right times, with reference to the right objects, towards the right people, with the right motive, and in the right way"* (LE p.147).
- *"men are good in but one way, but bad in many."* (LE p.147)

> **THE DEFINITION. Memorise it word for word:** *"Virtue, then, is a state of character concerned with choice, lying in a mean, i.e. the mean relative to us, this being determined by a rational principle, and by that principle by which the man of practical wisdom would determine it."* (LE p.147; L8 s23)

The definition holds the whole theory: *state of character* (genus), *choice* (prohairesis), *mean relative to us* (particularism), *rational principle* (the human function), *man of practical wisdom* (phronesis and the moral exemplar). Unpack it clause by clause in any virtue answer.

**12. Not everything has a mean** (LE p.147; L8 s24, labelled "Reductio ad absurdum"). Spite, shamelessness, envy, adultery, theft and murder are bad by name. *"however they are done they are wrong; for in general there is neither a mean of excess and deficiency, nor excess and deficiency of a mean."* This answers the objection that the doctrine of the mean permits "moderate murder".

**13. Table of virtues** (LE p.148; L8 s25):

| Sphere | Deficiency | **Mean** | Excess |
|---|---|---|---|
| Fear and confidence | Cowardice | **Courage** | Rashness |
| Bodily pleasures | Insensibility | **Temperance** | Self-indulgence |
| Giving and taking money (small sums) | Meanness | **Liberality** | Prodigality |
| Money (large sums) | Niggardliness | **Magnificence** | Tastelessness / vulgarity |
| Great honour | Undue humility | **Proper pride** | Empty vanity |
| Small honour | Unambitiousness | (nameless) | Ambition |
| Anger | Unirascibility | **Good temper** | Irascibility |

*Note: the L8 s10 list (modesty between shamelessness and bashfulness; temperance between "excess and abstinence") uses looser labels than the reading. In the exam, prefer the reading's terms.*

**14. How the extremes relate** (LE pp.148–149; L8 s26):
- The brave person *looks* rash to the coward and cowardly to the rash person.
- The extremes are more opposed to each other than to the mean.
- Sometimes one extreme is "more opposed" to the mean, for two reasons: because of the thing itself (rashness resembles courage more than cowardice does), and because of ourselves (we naturally lean toward pleasure, so self-indulgence is the bigger enemy of temperance).

**15. Practical advice for hitting the mean** (LE pp.149–150):
- *"anyone can get angry—that is easy… but to do this to the right person, to the right extent, at the right time, with the right motive, and in the right way, that is not for everyone, nor is it easy"*
- Move away from the more opposed extreme (Calypso: *"Hold the ship out beyond that surf and spray"*). As a second best, *"take the least of the evils"*.
- Pull yourself toward the opposite of your own tendency, *"as people do in straightening sticks that are bent"*.
- Guard against pleasure.
- In the end, *"the decision rests with perception"* (LE p.150), not with a rule.

**16. Practical wisdom (phronesis)** (L8 s11, s14, s27):
- It is *know-how* for acting so that the virtue is accomplished. It is gained through *life experience*.
- It can even require lying in a given situation (L8 s11). That contrasts sharply with Kant's exceptionless perfect duties.
- **The mean is not mediocrity:** *"'the mean' refers not to the mediocre or to the average but to the peak of excellence that is just right"* (L8 s27). As Aristotle says, *"with regard to what is best and right an extreme"* (LE p.147).

**17. The five intellectual virtues** (L8 s28). These are asked as a list:
1. **Episteme** (science): demonstrative knowledge of the necessary and eternal; links effects to causes.
2. **Techne** (art): knowing how to make or do things.
3. **Phronesis** (prudence or practical wisdom): knowing how to *act*, how to secure the ends of human life.
4. **Nous** (intuition or understanding): grasping the first principles from which demonstrations proceed.
5. **Sophia** (wisdom): nous and episteme combined.

**18. How intellectual and moral virtue relate** (L8 s27). This is the second half of 2024 Q4:
- Moral virtue is the *mean determined by a rational principle… as the man of practical wisdom would determine it*. So **moral virtue depends on phronesis**, an intellectual virtue. *"The intellectual virtue of prudence… is crucial to the acquisition of moral virtues"* (L8 s27).
- The dependence runs the other way too: phronesis develops only through habituated experience of acting well. Moral virtue gets the *end* right, and phronesis finds the right *means in the situation*. *(The two-way dependence is a standard reading of Aristotle, consistent with L8 s11 and s27. The excerpt does not develop it.)*
- **Prudence** is also one of the four **cardinal virtues**, along with courage, temperance and justice (L8 s27).

**19. Moral exemplars** (L8 s29–32): we learn virtue by emulating admirable people. Examples:
- Odysseus as an all-round excellent person (*aretē*, Kitto)
- Vitruvius's educated architect
- Heinlein's *"Specialization is for insects."*

These connect to engineering: the ideal engineer should be more than a technician (compare L9's critique of separatism).

**20. The modern revival** (L8 s7):
- **Anscombe** (1958): we need an account of *what a virtue is* before we speak of justice.
- **Foot:** the centrality of human well-being.
- **MacIntyre**, *After Virtue* (1981).
- **Hursthouse:** *"An action is right iff it is what a virtuous agent would characteristically do in the circumstances"* (*On Virtue Ethics*, 1999, quoted L8 s7).
- **Vallor:** *Technology and the Virtues* (2016).

### B. Objections and replies (Shafer-Landau Ch.10, LE pp.139–143; L8 slides)
1. **Inadequate guidance / conflict of virtues.** Honesty says tell your friend about her husband, but being a busybody is a vice. Virtue ethicists offer *"very little instruction"* for resolving this (Shafer-Landau, LE p.140). **Reply:** that is the point of phronesis and the inexactness thesis (LE p.144).
2. **Who are the exemplars?** Shafer-Landau's example: Churchill's racism kept him from recognising Gandhi as an exemplar (LE p.140).
3. **The priority problem.** Are acts right *because* the virtuous do them, or do the virtuous do them *because* they're right? This is a **Euthyphro-style dilemma** (compare L2 s17–20). Shafer-Landau calls it the theory's *"greatest"* vulnerability (LE p.142–143).
4. **Circularity** (Aristotle's own puzzle). Answered by the three conditions (LE pp.145–146).
5. **Doctrine of the mean as moderation.** Answered by "mean relative to us" and by "no mean of murder".

### C. Reading guide: Aristotle, LE pp. 143–150 (*NE* Bk II, trans. Ross)
- **Thesis:** moral virtue is a **state of character, acquired by habituation, concerned with choice, consisting in a mean relative to us as determined by practical wisdom.** Ethics aims at *becoming good*, not theorizing.
- **Order of the excerpt:** how virtue is acquired (habit, not nature) → ethics is inexact → excess, defect and the mean → pleasure and pain as signs, and education → the three conditions → genus (state of character) → differentia (mean) → the definition → things with no mean → the table → opposition of extremes → practical advice.

## PART 5: Lecture 9, Ethics, Engineering, and Responsibility (with reading vdP Ch.1)

### A. Concepts
**1. Responsibility** means *"being held accountable for your actions and for the effects of your actions."* Failing to act also counts: *"Failing to save a child who is drowning is therefore also a type of action."* (vdP p.9). The L9 s10 definition: the person is *"subject to responses such as blame, praise, punishment, or reward"*.

**2. Causal vs moral responsibility** (L9 s5): an O-ring, an algorithm or a self-driving car can be a *cause* without being *morally* responsible. **AI angle:** does a machine meet the knowledge and freedom conditions? Compare L7 s21: machines have functions, but do they have intentions?

**3. Active vs passive responsibility** (vdP p.9; L9 s11):
- **Active (forward-looking):** *"responsibility before something has happened. It refers to a duty or task to care for certain state-of-affairs or persons."* It is *"not primarily about blame but requires a certain positive attitude or character trait… Philosophers call such positive attitudes or character traits virtues"* (vdP p.13). That is a direct bridge to Aristotle.
- **Passive (backward-looking):** *"applicable after something (undesirable) has happened"* (vdP p.9).

**4. The three forms of passive responsibility** (vdP pp.10–11; L9 s15):
1. **Accountability:** being held to account for, or having to justify, one's actions towards those entitled to demand it. Example: *"NASA had to be able to render account… to the families of the victims, to society, and to the sitting judge"* (vdP p.11).
2. **Blameworthiness:** being *"a proper target of blame"* for one's actions or their consequences.
3. **Liability:** legal responsibility, such as damages or punishment (L9 s15: "Legal action").

*Relationship (vdP p.28):* *"If you are not able to give a satisfactory account you are blameworthy."* Accountability leads to blameworthiness when the account fails.

**5. Four conditions of blameworthiness** (vdP pp.11–13; L9 s16–20). **This was 2024 Q1.** *"The extent to which you can be blamed is determined by the degree to which these conditions are fulfilled"* (vdP p.11). So **blame comes in degrees**. Say this.

| Condition | Meaning (vdP) | Challenger application (vdP pp.11–13) | Link to earlier lectures |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Wrong-doing** | Violating a norm (legal, organisational, or *moral*) | NASA reversed the **burden of proof**: it demanded proof the launch was *unsafe*, though its own policy required proof that it was *safe* | Needs an ethical framework to supply the norm: Kant, utilitarianism or virtue ethics (vdP p.11, L9 s16) |
| **Causal contribution** | A necessary contribution to the outcome. **Failing to act counts too.** Usually not sufficient by itself | Both NASA's project team and Thiokol management had to give a positive decision, and either could have stopped it. Boisjoly could perhaps have gone to the press or intervened earlier | Act vs omission; Singer's pond |
| **Foreseeability** | Could reasonably have known the consequences. Not blamed if *"totally unreasonable to expect"* awareness (vdP p.12) | All parties knew O-ring erosion was a risk at low temperatures. There was no conclusive proof, but enough to foresee danger | Utilitarian foresight problem (L6 s12). Collingridge dilemma (L9 s31) |
| **Freedom** | Not acting under compulsion. Pressure **reduces** responsibility but doesn't **remove** it | Pressures: schedule, the public interest in McAuliffe, Thiokol's contract, Boisjoly's job. *"probably not strong enough"* to remove freedom, though Boisjoly's responsibility is arguably *diminished* (vdP pp.12–13) | **Ought implies can** (L9 s19; L1–2). Hypothetical vs categorical imperatives (L9 s19) |

**6. Roles and responsibilities** (vdP pp.9–10; L9 s12–14). **This was 2024 Q3, first part.**
- **Role responsibility:** *"Each role brings with it certain responsibilities"* (vdP p.9). Roles include friend, parent, citizen, employee, engineer, expert and colleague.
- Roles can be **formal** (laid down in law, contracts, professional codes) or **informal** (family, friends) (vdP p.10; L9 s13).
- **Role conflict:** Boisjoly was both an **employee** (expected to be loyal to his superiors) and an **engineer** (expected to give technically sound, safety-minded advice) (vdP p.10).
- **Moral responsibility** *"is based on moral obligations, moral norms, or moral duties"*. It **can extend beyond roles** (Boisjoly's care for the astronauts) and **can limit roles** (*"Think of the role of Mafioso"*) (vdP p.10).
- **Professional responsibility** is *"based on one's role as professional in as far it stays within the limits of what is morally allowed"* (vdP p.10). So morality bounds professional role. That is the key inference for 2024 Q3.
- L9 s12 adds **W.D. Ross: *prima facie* duties vs duties all things considered** (*sans phrase*). A role gives *prima facie* duties. When they conflict, you must judge what is right *all things considered*.

**7. Bovens's five features of active responsibility** (vdP p.13; L9 s21). The slides attach a theory to each:

| Bovens feature | L9 s21 says "Recall…" |
|---|---|
| Adequate perception of threatened violations of norms | Virtue ethics (perception and phronesis) |
| Consideration of the consequences | Consequentialism |
| Autonomy: making one's own independent moral decisions | (Kant, autonomy) |
| Conduct based on a verifiable and consistent code | Kantian deontology |
| Taking role obligations seriously | Aristotle's virtue ethics |

**These five features give you a ready-made way to link all three theories inside an engineering answer.**

**8. Ideals of engineers** (vdP pp.13–21). *Ideals* are *"particularly motivating and inspiring"* and aim at *"an optimum or maximum"* (vdP p.13).

| Ideal | Content | Moral status (vdP) | Course example |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Technological enthusiasm** | Developing new possibilities. Florman: *"the existential pleasures of engineering"* (p.14) | Not wrong in itself, since intrinsic motivation is good. The danger is overlooking negative effects | Google Earth (privacy, security); **Wernher von Braun**: *"My refusal to join the party would have meant that I would have had to abandon the work of my life. Therefore, I decided to join"* (p.15) |
| **Effectiveness and efficiency** | Effectiveness = how far a goal is achieved. Efficiency = goal achieved ÷ effort (p.16) | Seems "neutral and objective" but is **value-laden**: it presupposes an external goal, which *"can be… war or even genocide"*, and the choice of inputs is value-laden too (p.18) | **Taylor** and scientific management. **Eichmann**, *"proud of the efficient way"* he contributed to genocide (Arendt) (p.18) |
| **Human welfare** | *"Engineers shall hold paramount the safety, health and welfare of the public"* (codes, p.19). Safety is central. For software, privacy and reliability | Morally laudable. It shows engineering *"is not something that is morally neutral and that engineers do more than merely develop neutral means for the goals of others"* (p.21) | **Johan van Veen** ("the new Cassandra") and the Dutch dykes / 1953 flood (pp.19–20) |

**9. Engineers vs managers: three models** (vdP pp.21–25; L9 s27–28):
- **Separatism:** *"scientists and engineers should apply the technical inputs, but appropriate management and political organs should make the value decisions"* (Goldberg, quoted vdP p.21).
  - The **tripartite model** illustrates it: politicians and managers / engineers / users. Engineers are responsible only for technical creation.
  - Boisjoly after the disaster: *"I had my say"*.
  - The danger is the **"hired gun"**. Tom Lehrer's lyric about von Braun: *"Once the rockets go up / Who cares where they come down / 'that's not my department'"* (vdP p.22).
- **Technocracy** (Taylor): government by experts. Problems: (i) engineers have no special expertise about goals or acceptable risk (the *technocratic fallacy*); (ii) it is **undemocratic and paternalistic**, and paternalism *"denies that people have the right to shape their own lives"* and clashes with **moral autonomy** (vdP p.23). That is a Kantian point.
- **Whistle-blowing:** disclosing abuses *"without the consent of his/her superiors"* to remedy them or warn the public (vdP p.24).
  - Example: **Inez Austin** (Hanford).
  - **De George's five conditions** (vdP p.24): (1) serious harm to the public; (2) reported to the immediate superior without effect; (3) internal procedures exhausted; (4) evidence that would convince a reasonable, impartial observer; (5) good reason to believe disclosure will prevent the harm at reasonable cost.
  - Problems: **sacrifice** and **limited effect**, because communication has already broken down (vdP p.25).
- **vdP's preferred alternative:** a cooperative model where engineers *"think about broader issues than just engineering decisions but do not decide on these issues alone"* (vdP p.29).

**10. The social context of technology** (vdP pp.25–28; L9 s29–32):
- **Actors:** developers and producers, users, regulators.
- **Stakeholders:** have an interest but can't necessarily steer the technology. Morality says *don't neglect them because they are powerless*.
- **Unpredictability:** Teflon.
- **The Collingridge dilemma:** early on, consequences can't be predicted. Later, the technology is entrenched and hard to change. **Constructive Technology Assessment** (CTA) runs alongside development and feeds back into design.
- **Net effect on responsibility:**
  - It **diminishes** responsibility, since there are many actors and causal contribution and foreseeability are reduced.
  - It **extends** responsibility, since engineers must involve stakeholders (vdP p.28; L9 s32).

**11. Extra lecture-only material** (L9 s26, s33–37):
- *"If engineering involves means-ends reasoning, then ethics involves ends-means reasoning"*, so **ethics is more foundational than engineering** (L9 s26).
- India's "social conscience of science": Dhruv Raina; Amulya Reddy's **ASTRA** (*"a weapon (or asthra in Sanskrit) in the interests of the poor"*, Reddy 1988, L9 s34).
- Edgerton, "In Praise of Luddism" (2011).
- **Arendt on Eichmann:** *"Could the activity of thinking as such… be among the conditions that make men abstain from evil-doing"* ("Thinking and Moral Considerations", 1971, L9 s36). It connects to von Braun and the hired gun, and to Aristotle (the thoughtless person lacks phronesis).

### B. Reading guide: vdP Ch.1
- **Thesis:** engineers carry **both passive and active responsibility.** Their professional responsibility is bounded by morality and includes the ideal of human welfare. Neither separatism, technocracy nor routine whistle-blowing handles the engineer–manager tension well. The social context both narrows and widens engineers' responsibility.
- **Main case:** Challenger, used to illustrate *every* condition. **Supporting cases:** von Braun (enthusiasm, hired gun), Taylor (efficiency, technocracy), van Veen (welfare, near-whistle-blower), Inez Austin (whistle-blowing), Teflon (unpredictability).

## PART 8: How to write answers for this examiner

### 8.1 Template for a 10-mark answer (300–400 words, about 17 minutes)
1. **Opening (2–3 lines):** define the key term *in the course's words* and place it in the framework ("Kant's is a theory of the right…"). That earns a point immediately.
2. **Body: one labelled block per sub-question.** Use the question's own parts as headings: (a), (b), (c). The examiner counts points, so make each point visible.
3. **Lists as numbered items** with one explanatory clause each. Never give the bare list.
4. **One example per concept.** Prefer course examples (Challenger, shopkeeper, Milo, von Braun).
5. **One short quotation or precise paraphrase per answer.** Name the author and work in the sentence itself ("As Aristotle puts it in Book II of the *Nicomachean Ethics*, …").
6. **Objection and reply (2–3 lines)** wherever the question says evaluate, critically, or would you. That is where "inferences you draw" are scored.
7. **Closing line that commits to a position** or states the main inference.

### 8.2 Using quotations naturally
- **Fold the quotation into your own sentence** instead of setting it apart: *Kant holds that the good will's worth is unaffected by results; even if it achieved nothing, "like a jewel, it would still shine by itself."*
- **Only quote what you then use.** Follow each quotation with "This means…" or "Hence…".
- Keep quotations **under about 25 words**. Quote the *key formulation* (the CI, FH, the definition of virtue). Paraphrase the rest.
- If you aren't sure of the exact words, **paraphrase without quote marks.** An exact paraphrase earns the same point.

### 8.4 Common mistakes
- Saying Kant **forbids using people as means.** He forbids using them *merely* as means.
- Treating the **mean as moderation or average.** It is "relative to us" and "an extreme with regard to what is best".
- Saying **intellectual virtue comes from habit.** It comes from *teaching*. Moral virtue comes from *habit*.
- Mixing up **accountability and liability.** Liability is *legal*.
- Treating **freedom as all-or-nothing.** Pressure *reduces* responsibility but doesn't remove it.
- Calling **efficiency value-neutral.** vdP says it is value-laden.
- Mixing up **act and rule utilitarianism** on the patient's dilemma. AU may permit harvesting; RU forbids it because a rule permitting it isn't optimific.
- Calling the **Golden Rule** Kant's test. L7 s11 rejects it because it depends on what a person happens to desire.
- Attributing **"consequentialism"** to Bentham. Anscombe coined it. Attributing **the trolley problem's name** to Foot. Thomson named it; Foot introduced the case.

**Model Answer 1.** *How do roles and responsibilities relate? How would a utilitarian, a Kantian and a virtue ethicist respond when moral responsibility conflicts with professional responsibility? Illustrate with a real example.*

**Roles and responsibilities.** van de Poel and Royakkers note that "each role brings with it certain responsibilities": as employee, engineer, expert, colleague, parent. These role responsibilities can be formal (contracts, professional codes) or informal (family). Because we occupy several roles at once, they can conflict. Moral responsibility, which rests on moral norms and duties, is not confined to roles. It can **extend beyond** them and can **limit** them; no one has a genuine responsibility to perform the role of a Mafioso. Hence professional responsibility is role-based responsibility only *"in as far it stays within the limits of what is morally allowed."* In W.D. Ross's terms, a role yields *prima facie* duties, which must be weighed against duties all things considered.

**Example: Challenger (1986).** Roger Boisjoly, as a Morton Thiokol **employee**, was expected to be loyal to managers who wanted the launch to go ahead for contractual reasons. As an **engineer**, he was expected to give safety-minded technical advice: the O-rings were untested below 11°C. Management overrode him, and seven astronauts died.

**(a) Utilitarian.** The right act maximises overall well-being. Weighing seven lives and public trust against a contract and a delayed schedule, an act utilitarian requires refusing to launch and, if necessary, escalating. A rule utilitarian reaches the same verdict through an optimific rule such as "engineers must hold public safety paramount". *Limit:* the verdict depends on foreseeing consequences. Boisjoly lacked conclusive data, so the outcome is uncertain.

**(b) Kantian.** Moral duty is categorical. Loyalty to an employer is only hypothetical ("if you want to keep your job…"). The maxim "certify as safe what I believe may be unsafe, to secure a contract" cannot be universalized, because certification would lose its meaning. It also treats the astronauts *merely as a means*, since they could not consent to a concealed risk. Boisjoly must not endorse the launch whatever the cost to him.

**(c) Virtue ethicist.** The question becomes what an honest, courageous and practically wise engineer would *characteristically do*. Courage is the mean between cowardice (silence) and rashness (reckless sabotage). Phronesis would push him to raise concerns *earlier*, use internal channels, and, following De George's conditions, go public if those failed. This matches Bovens's view of active responsibility as a *virtue*.

**Conclusion.** All three theories put the moral above the merely professional-as-employee role, for different reasons: outcomes, duty, character. Boisjoly's freedom was limited by pressure, which reduces his blameworthiness, but his responsibility was not removed.

**Model Answer 2.** *How does virtue ethics work? What is virtue and how is it acquired according to Aristotle? Describe the intellectual and moral virtues and their relation.*

**How virtue ethics works.** Utilitarianism evaluates consequences and Kant evaluates motives. Virtue ethics evaluates **character**. It shifts the question from "what should I do?" to "how should I live?" Right action is what a virtuous agent would characteristically do (Hursthouse), so we act well by *becoming* good people. Its three components are **eudaimonia** (flourishing), **virtue** and **phronesis**. By Aristotle's function argument, a thing's excellence makes it do its work well (the eye sees well), and since the human function is rational activity, human virtue is the state that makes us live rationally well.

**What virtue is.** Aristotle argues by genus and differentia. The soul has passions, faculties and states of character. Virtue isn't a passion, because we aren't praised for merely feeling fear and passions involve no choice. It isn't a faculty, because faculties come by nature. So virtue is a **state of character**, specifically a disposition to choose the mean: *"a state of character concerned with choice, lying in a mean, i.e. the mean relative to us… determined by… the man of practical wisdom."* The mean is "relative to us": six pounds of food is too little for Milo and too much for a beginner. Courage lies between cowardice and rashness, liberality between meanness and prodigality. Some acts, like murder and theft, have no mean.

**How it is acquired.** Virtues arise "neither by nature, nor contrary to nature": we are adapted by nature to receive them and perfected by **habit**. A stone can't be trained to rise, but we "become just by doing just acts", as builders become builders by building. To answer the apparent circularity, a truly virtuous act also requires that the agent (1) has knowledge, (2) chooses the act for its own sake, and (3) acts from a firm and unchangeable character. Taking **pleasure** in the act is the sign that the virtue has formed, hence the importance of upbringing.

**Intellectual vs moral virtues.** Intellectual virtue grows from **teaching** and needs experience and time. Moral virtue grows from **habit** (*ēthikē* from *ethos*). The five intellectual virtues are episteme, techne, phronesis, nous and sophia. Moral virtues include courage, temperance, liberality and justice.

**Their relation.** The definition itself joins them: the mean is fixed by the rational principle of the person of **practical wisdom**. Without phronesis, a good disposition can't find the right act "at the right time, towards the right people, in the right way". Phronesis in turn matures only through habituated experience. So moral virtue sets the right end, and phronesis finds the right means in each situation.

---
**Model Answer 3.** *Distinguish "means to an end" from "end in itself" with an example of each. How are fairness, consistency and humanity accounted for in Kantian ethics?*

**Means vs end in itself.** Something is a *means* when it is valued for what it brings about. Its worth is conditional, like a tool, money or a hypothetical imperative ("if you want the job, take the course"). An *end in itself* has unconditional worth, independent of anyone's desires. For Kant, *"rational beings are called persons because their nature already marks them out as an end in itself,"* whereas non-rational beings "have only a relative worth, as means, and are therefore called things." Example of a means: a calculator I use to finish my assignment. Example of an end in itself: the friend I help study, whose own goals I adopt as mine. Kant doesn't forbid using people as means. I use a shopkeeper to buy groceries. What he forbids is using anyone *merely* as a means, in a way they *"cannot possibly agree to"*.

**Consistency.** Kant's first formulation: *"act only in accordance with that maxim through which you can at the same time will that it become a universal law."* Test: formulate your maxim, imagine everyone acting on it, and ask whether your goal is still achievable. A lying promise fails: if everyone promised falsely, no one would believe promises, so the maxim "would have to destroy itself." Universal plagiarism would likewise make assessment meaningless. An inconsistent maxim is irrational, which is why for Kant immorality is irrational.

**Fairness.** When we do wrong, Kant notes, we don't really want our maxim to be universal law. *"we take the liberty of making an exception to it for ourselves (or just for this once)."* Universalizability forbids this free-riding: it is unfair to profit from a rule while expecting everyone else to keep it. Consistency and fairness are thus the two criteria of universalizability.

**Humanity.** Consistency alone might allow tyranny of the majority or slavery, so Kant adds: *"So act that you use humanity, whether in your own person or in the person of any other, always at the same time as an end, never merely as a means."* Humanity, meaning our rational, **autonomous** capacity to legislate our own ends (*autos* + *nomos*), has intrinsic worth. Deception and coercion violate it because they bypass the other person's rational agency. In the patient's dilemma, harvesting the healthy man's organs uses him merely as a means, however good the outcome.

**Evaluation.** The formulations explain why rights can't be traded for utility. Critics argue that Kant cannot account for animals, which aren't rational, and that his perfect duties are too rigid when consequences are severe.
